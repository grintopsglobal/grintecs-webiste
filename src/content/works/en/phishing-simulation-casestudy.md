Over two months, the Grintec team sent a series of simulated phishing emails to a company's employees. The goal was simple: measure how ready staff are for a real phishing attack, without actually harming anyone.

## At a glance

This program is *Phishing Simulation as a Service*, a series of controlled phishing campaigns designed to train and measure employee vigilance. Any data "captured" was stored only for evaluation and never misused.

The results reveal a pattern common to many organizations. Employees are cautious enough not to click carelessly, but the culture of reporting suspicious emails still needs strengthening.

## Scope

The simulation ran over two months across two campaign types:

- **General phishing**, mass emails disguised as notifications from popular services, such as account notices and online meeting invitations.
- **Spear phishing**, targeted emails tailored per department, some impersonating requests from internal colleagues.

## Key results

![Phishing simulation funnel: 60.6% opened, 7.0% clicked, 5.6% submitted data, 36.6% reported](/uploads/works/phishing-simulation-casestudy/01-results.webp)

More than six in ten emails were opened (60.6%), a sign the lures looked convincing. The good news: only a few went on to click a link (7.0%) or submit data (5.6%). Still, that 5.6% who submitted credentials matters. A single leaked account is enough to give an attacker a way in.

## Anatomy of the most effective attack

The most "successful" campaign was not the mass email but the spear phishing that impersonated an internal colleague with a request that sounded routine and urgent.

![Spear phishing anatomy: email from a fake colleague, urgent request, fake login page, credentials captured](/uploads/works/phishing-simulation-casestudy/02-anatomy.webp)

The pattern is simple but effective. The sender name is spoofed to look like a coworker, the message carries a routine-sounding request, for example asking someone to review a document, and the victim is steered to a fake login page on a deliberately lookalike domain. The moment an email and password are typed, the credentials are captured. This is why spear phishing is far more dangerous than mass phishing. It attacks trust, not just curiosity.

## The reporting gap

![Reporting gap: 36.6% reported against a 50% target](/uploads/works/phishing-simulation-casestudy/03-reporting.webp)

Reporting a phishing email is the last and cheapest line of defense. Yet only 36.6% of recipients reported it, still below the 50% minimum target. That means many employees recognized a suspicious email but chose to stay silent. One quick report can protect the entire organization.

## Two layers of defense

Each finding was paired with layered recommendations, strengthening the human side and the technical side together.

![Recommendations: strengthen the human side and the technical side](/uploads/works/phishing-simulation-casestudy/04-recommendations.webp)

On the human side, the keys are regular awareness training, a one-click report button to make reporting easy, recognition for those who report, and regular simulations, especially spear phishing. On the technical side, enable MFA on all accounts so a leaked credential does not immediately become a breach, deploy email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC), scan and rewrite links, and block lookalike domains.

## Lessons

- **Convincing lures still get through.** A 60.6% open rate shows modern phishing is hard to tell from the real thing.
- **Spear phishing attacks trust.** Impersonating a colleague is far more effective than a mass email.
- **MFA is the safety net.** If credentials can leak, MFA keeps a leak from becoming a breach.
- **Reporting is a defense, not a formality.** A reporting culture below 50% is a gap worth closing.
